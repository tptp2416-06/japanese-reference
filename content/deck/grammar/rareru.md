先生が生徒をほめた — the teacher praised the student. Change who the sentence is about, and it becomes 生徒が先生にほめられた — the student was praised by the teacher.

That's what られる does: it moves the sentence onto the person or thing something happens to. The one who did the praising, if it's named at all, now takes に instead of が.

母が晩ご飯を作った — Mom made dinner — can change shape too: 母が私に晩ご飯を作らせた — Mom had me make dinner.

That's させる: it adds a person who makes or lets someone else act. Mom is still the one in charge; I'm the one who ends up cooking, and I'm the one marked with に this time.

Both endings use に, for opposite reasons. られる's に names who acted on you; させる's に names who was made or let to act. The two are easy to mix up once they sit side by side.

The two endings can also stack. 母に晩ご飯を作らせられた — I was made by Mom to make dinner. That combination is させられる, reporting Mom's causative from my side, as something that happened to me rather than something I chose.

The page starts with られる by itself — first the plain, neutral use, then the two flavors where the event happens to you rather than because of you, then the quoting use in 言われた.

させる comes next on its own, then させられる where the two combine, then される — the same combination said in fewer syllables, and no less common for it.

@@ tldr

られる puts the subject on the receiving end of an action; させる adds someone who makes or lets another person act. Combined, させられる reports being made to do something you didn't choose.
Decide whose side of the event you're telling first — the actor, or the one it happened to — then, if you're the one arranging someone else's action, whether you're forcing it or allowing it.

@@ neutral-passive — the plain, uninvolved passive

This is られる at its plainest, before inconvenience, an added causer, or a quote gets layered on top of it.

**Form:** `(agent に) + V passive`

- **The one acted on becomes the subject** — exactly as in the opening example, with the actor, if named, taking に instead of が.
- **No inconvenience is implied here** — it just reports that an action happened, usually to a thing rather than a person, and usually without much interest in who did it.
- **The agent is often left out entirely** — この橋は明治時代に作られた — this bridge was built in the Meiji era — doesn't name a builder, and doesn't need to.
- **Negating it is ordinary** — この製品はもう作られていない — this product isn't made anymore — is a completely normal thing to read on a package or a listing.

### More common in writing than in speech

Neutral passive turns up constantly on signs, in product descriptions, and in historical writing, where the fact matters more than anyone's particular involvement.

Conversation reaches for it far less. A speaker is more likely to just state the fact directly, or name who did it, unless leaving the doer vague is actually doing some work.

### One shape, several jobs

Ichidan verbs and 来る don't have a separate word for "can." The same られる ending covers passive, potential, and honorific meaning, and only context sorts them out.

| reading | what 見られる means here |
|---|---|
| passive (this page) | is looked at, gets seen |
| potential (ichidan · 来る only) | can see, is able to see |
| honorific | a respected person looks, sees |

Godan verbs don't share this overlap — their potential form is built differently (飲める, never 飲まれる), so passive and potential never collide for them.

-> Contrast: [[kanoukei-koto-ga-dekiru#kanoukei]] — the potential reading of this same shape
-> Contrast: [[reru-rareru]] — the honorific reading, used to describe a respected person's own actions politely

@@ adversity-passive — something happened, and it got to you

This is the same られる as above, in a different mood.

**Form:** `agent に + object を + V passive`

- **The subject is the one affected, not the one acting** — 妹に日記を読まれた reports that my little sister read my diary, told from my side, as something that got to me.
- **The base verb's object stays exactly where it was** — 日記 keeps its を here just as it would in the active sentence; only the subject and the verb change.
- **Negating it works the same as any other verb** — 妹に日記を読まれなかった just states that the reading didn't happen; the reading of られる itself doesn't shift.

```eg
plain: 妹[いもうと]が私[わたし]の日記[にっき]を読[よ]んだ | My little sister read my diary.
affected: 妹[いもうと]に日記[にっき]を読[よ]まれた | My little sister read my diary, and it got to me.
```

@@ intransitive-adversity — an event that reaches you anyway

The same adversity reading reaches even verbs that never had an object to lose.

**Form:** `agent に + V passive` (no object)

- **Some intransitive verbs never had an object to keep, and られる still attaches** — 隣の部屋の人に夜中に騒がれた — the people next door were noisy in the middle of the night — adds the person who had to put up with the noise; 騒ぐ itself never took an object.
- **This is where an event with no room for a person suddenly gets one** — the base sentence has no slot for who was affected; られる is what creates it.

!!! note "What's in scope"
    雨が降った — it rained — has no room for a person at all — it's just weather happening. られる adds one: 雨に降られた makes space for someone the rain affected, without changing what 降る itself means.

### No object to begin with

Plenty of intransitive verbs work the same way as 降る — nothing about the base sentence changes shape; られる just adds the affected person, with nothing else to reassign.

```eg
object kept: 妹[いもうと]に日記[にっき]を読[よ]まれた | My little sister read my diary, and it got to me.
no object: 電車[でんしゃ]で子供[こども]に泣[な]かれた | A child cried on me on the train.
```

```dialog
note: A's comment about looking tired licenses B's adversity passive — B explains what caused it, not just states a fact.
A: 眠[ねむ]そうだね。 | You look sleepy.
B: 隣[となり]の部屋[へや]の人[ひと]に、夜中[よなか]に騒[さわ]がれたんだ。 | The people in the next room were noisy in the middle of the night.
```

!!! warning "English Trap"
    An English passive doesn't automatically call for られる. If the verb already has an intransitive partner, that plain verb often already carries what the English passive was reaching for, with no doer needed anywhere in the sentence.

    "The meeting started late" doesn't need 始められた — 会議が始まった already reports the starting, with nobody's identity in the sentence at all. Reach for られる only when who did it, or who it affected, is actually part of what you want to say.

-> Compare: [[transitivity]] — choosing between the plain intransitive and the passive when nobody's identity needs to be in the sentence

@@ iwareta — reporting what you were told

One more られる job before the page turns to させる: reporting what someone said.

**Form:** `[quoted content] と/って + 言われた`

- **This reports what someone told you, using the passive of 言う** — the person who said it, if named, takes に.
- **と is the neutral quote marker; って is the casual one** — the rest of the sentence doesn't have to change register to match.

-> Compare: [[tte#quote]] — って's own page, the same quoting job at casual register

### The quoted words stay the speaker's own

Everything before と or って belongs to the person being quoted, not to you. 言われた only marks that it reached you as something said — it doesn't touch the content of what was said.

```ex
母[はは]に、九時[くじ]までに帰[かえ]ってきなさいと言[い]われた。
en: I was told by my mom to be home by nine.
lit: was said "come home by nine" to, by mother
note: Everything up to と is my mother's own wording; 言われた just marks that it reached me as something said.
bd: 母に、|by my mother / 九時までに帰ってきなさいと*|"be home by nine" / 言われた|was told
pat: quoted command + と + 言われる, passive report
```

### In conversation

言われた and its relatives show up constantly once the missing details are obvious from context:

- 財布、取られた！ — My wallet got stolen!
- また待たされたよ。 — Kept waiting again.
- 言われた通りにしただけ。 — I just did what I was told.

None of these name who did it. Casual speech drops the agent whenever it's already obvious, leaving just the affected party and the passive verb to carry the whole sentence.

@@ saseru — making, or letting, someone act

With られる's jobs now in view, here's the other half of the pair.

**Form:** `causee に + V causative` (the base verb already has an object) · `causee を + V causative` (it doesn't)

- **The causee gets に when the verb already has an object** — 先生が生徒に本を読ませた — the teacher had the student read the book — keeps を on 本 and moves the causee to に.
- **The causee gets を when the verb has no object of its own** — 子供を歩かせた — I made the child walk — since 歩く never had an object to begin with.
- **The same ending can mean "make" or "let"** — nothing in させる itself decides which. The rest of the sentence does.
- **Negating させる usually withholds permission, not force** — 子供にゲームをさせない — I won't let the child play games — not as refusing to force it.

```dialog
note: The same させた could mean force or permission; a birthday makes it read as permission here.
A: 子供[こども]にお菓子[かし]を食[た]べさせたの？ | Did you let the kids have candy?
B: うん、誕生日[たんじょうび]だから。 | Yeah, since it's his birthday.
```

!!! warning "Pitfall"
    させる doesn't grammatically choose between "make" and "let" — the same form covers both. What decides the reading is whether the situation makes force or permission the plausible one.

    Forcing medicine on a child who's fighting it reads as force. Letting a child have cake on their birthday reads as permission. The ending never changes.

```eg
make: 薬[くすり]を嫌[いや]がる子[こ]に薬[くすり]を飲[の]ませた | I made the child, who didn't want to, take the medicine.
let: 誕生日[たんじょうび]だからケーキを好[す]きなだけ食[た]べさせた | Since it was his birthday, I let him eat as much cake as he wanted.
```

### に marks two different roles

Both られる and させる mark the "other party" with に, and they're never the same party.

生徒が先生にほめられた — the student was praised by the teacher. Here に marks the person who acted on the subject.

先生が生徒に本を読ませた — the teacher made the student read the book. Here に marks the person who was made to act. Before assuming a に phrase means "by" or "for," check which of these two sentences you're actually in.

@@ saserareru — made to act, from your side

Stack the two together, and this is what results.

**Form:** `V causative stem + られる`

- **Someone else set it up, and you're the one who did it** — the causative-passive reports an action from the side of the person who had no real say in it.
- **It's the causative and the passive working together** — させる adds the person who made you act; られる reports the whole thing from your side, as something that happened to you.
- **The base verb's own object, if it has one, doesn't move** — only the subject and the verb change shape.

```ex
部長[ぶちょう]に日曜日[にちようび]も出勤[しゅっきん]させられた。
en: My boss made me come in even on Sundays.
lit: was made to come to work by the department head, even on Sunday
note: させ carries the "made to do it" force; られ reports the whole thing from the side of the person who had to comply.
bd: 部長に|by my boss / 日曜日も|even on Sundays / 出勤させられた*|was made to come to work
pat: causative stem + られる, causative-passive
```

### The causative-passive across verb classes

Both endings attach the same way to any verb, once you know which pattern to look for.

| verb class | dictionary form | passive (られる) | causative (させる) | causative-passive (full) | causative-passive (contracted) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| godan (う) | 買う — buy | 買われる — is bought | 買わせる — makes or lets someone buy | 買わせられる — is made to buy | 買わされる — is made to buy, one syllable shorter |
| godan (く) | 書く — write | 書かれる — is written | 書かせる — makes or lets someone write | 書かせられる — is made to write | 書かされる — is made to write, one syllable shorter |
| ichidan | 食べる — eat | 食べられる — is eaten | 食べさせる — makes or lets someone eat | 食べさせられる — is made to eat | — |
| する | 心配する — worry | 心配される — is worried about | 心配させる — makes someone worry | 心配させられる — is made to worry | — |
| 来る | 来る — come | 来られる — has someone come, unwanted | 来させる — makes someone come | 来させられる — is made to come | — |

The contracted column is empty for three of these five rows on purpose — not every verb can shorten this far. される, next, covers which ones do and why.

@@ sareru — made to act, said in fewer syllables

One syllable shorter, and just as common.

**Form:** `causative-passive, one syllable shorter` (godan verbs only)

- **される is what you'll actually hear** — casual conversation reaches for される far more than the full させられる, which is what dictionaries and textbooks show first.
- **Coming last here is about how it's built, not how common it is** — される is made from させられる, so it's taught after it; that says nothing about which one you'll actually hear more.
- **Godan verbs can drop a syllable from the full causative-passive** — 待たせられる shortens to 待たされる, keeping the same meaning.
- **Written Japanese and careful speech keep the full form** — される stays casual; formal writing doesn't contract it.

### Not every verb can shorten this far

Only godan verbs take the shortcut, and even they have one exception.

Ichidan verbs have no shorter form at all — there's no 食べされる next to 食べさせられる.

する doesn't contract either. Shortening 心配させられる would, in principle, land on 心配された — already the ordinary passive of する, meaning was worried about.

Avoiding that overlap is a reasonable explanation for why verbs built on する keep the full causative-passive.

Godan verbs ending in す are the other holdout. 話させられる could, in principle, shorten to 話さされる, but the doubled さ sound is awkward to say, and speakers generally keep the full form there too.

```eg
full: 兄[あに]に部屋[へや]の掃除[そうじ]を手伝[てつだ]わせられた | My brother made me help clean the room.
contracted: 兄[あに]に部屋[へや]の掃除[そうじ]を手伝[てつだ]わされた | My brother made me help clean the room.
```
