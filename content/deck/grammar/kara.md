から shows up in sentences that don't look like they have much in common. 忙しいから — because I'm busy — gives a reason; 大阪から — from Osaka — names a place something came from; 三時から — since three o'clock — names when something started.

One way to hold these three together: think of から as always marking a starting point. A reason is where an explanation starts; a place is where a journey starts; a time is when something starts.

That's a memory aid, not a strict rule. Nothing requires these three jobs to share a deeper grammatical root — each is learned and used on its own terms.

The question worth asking is which starting point you mean. Once you've decided that, から attaches the same way every time — directly after whatever comes before it, with no change of form required.

@@ tldr

から marks a starting point: of a reason, a place, or a time.
Attached to a clause it gives the reason for what follows; attached to a place or a time word it marks where something begins.
Decide which starting point you mean, then attach から directly — no change to the form of whatever comes before it.

@@ reason — から as your own reason

**Form:** `Clause (plain or polite) + から、result.` · `N／Na-adj + だから／ですから、result.`

- **から states the reason as your own claim, and takes the whole clause before it** — 電車が遅れているから — the train's running late, so — hands over everything before から as the justification, not just the last word of it.
- **Decide the reason first; から only marks where it ends** — once you know what you're crediting or blaming, から goes right after it and the result follows.
- **Nouns and na-adjectives need だ or です first** — 学生だから — because I'm a student — not 学生から.
- **Negating the reason clause only flips its polarity** — 忙しいから行けない — I can't go because I'm busy — and 忙しくないから行ける — I can go because I'm not busy — work the same way otherwise.
- **から is register-flexible on its own** — it attaches fine after です and ます. What usually signals formality is which connector gets chosen in the first place, covered next.

```ex
電車[でんしゃ]が遅[おく]れているから、会議[かいぎ]に少[すこ]し遅[おく]れます。
en: The train's running late, so I'll be a little late to the meeting.
note: から takes the whole preceding clause — the train being late — as its reason; a single noun couldn't stand in its place the same way.
bd: 電車が遅れている|the train is late / から、*|so / 会議に|to the meeting / 少し遅れます|I'll be a little late
pat: clause + から, result stated
reg: casual|電車が遅れてるから、ちょっと遅れる。 / polite|電車が遅れていますから、少し遅れます。
```

### Softening for formal speech

から doesn't get replaced because it stops being grammatical — it stays grammatical at any politeness level. It gets replaced because it sounds like a personal, assertive claim, and formal speech often wants a softer frame.

| form | example | effect | how it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| から | 頭が痛いから、会社を休みます。 — I have a headache, so I'll take the day off work. | states the reason as your own direct claim | very common in casual speech |
| [[node#reason]] | 頭が痛いので、会社を休みます。 — Since I have a headache, I'll take the day off work. | presents the reason as circumstance, not personal claim | neutral polite — the common default once things turn formal |
| ですから／ますから | 頭が痛いですから、会社を休みます。 — I have a headache, so I'll be taking the day off work. | keeps から, raises only the surrounding verb's politeness | polite, but still reads more direct than ので |
| ものですから | 頭が痛いものですから、会社を休みます。 — It's just that I have a headache, so I'll be taking the day off work. | frames the reason almost apologetically, as an explanation being offered | formal, explanatory |

!!! warning "Pitfall"
    から stays grammatical at any politeness level — it attaches fine to です and ます directly.

    What changes with formality is often the connector itself: speakers reach for ので, or a fuller frame like ものですから, rather than just politely inflecting the words around から.

### In conversation

んだから fuses explanatory んだ with から, framing the reason as something the listener should already be sensing rather than as new information.

-> Continue with: [[n-da#n-da-kara]] — んだ fused with から for a more pointed, explanatory reason

だから picks a reason up out of the air and opens a new sentence with it, instead of attaching directly to the clause that gave the reason.

-> Compare: [[da-kara#main]] — から turned into its own sentence-opening word

```dialog
note: B states both the reason and the result in full — nothing is left for A to work out, unlike から left standing alone.
A: どうしたの？元気[げんき]ないね。 | What's wrong? You seem down.
B: 昨夜[ゆうべ]よく眠[ねむ]れなかったから、ちょっと疲[つか]れてるんだ。 | I didn't sleep well last night, so I'm a bit tired.
```

@@ postposed — から left standing alone

**Form:** `[clause, plain or polite] + から。` — stands alone; the result is left unstated

- **から alone can carry the whole sentence** — a speaker states the reason and stops, trusting the listener to supply the result: 忙しいから — because I'm busy — can be a complete reply on its own.
- **The implied result comes from context, not from grammar** — から doesn't grammatically require anything after it; what's left unsaid is recovered from what was just said or asked.
- **Negating the reason only flips its polarity** — 忙しくないから — because I'm not busy — is still a postposed reason; nothing about the pattern itself changes.
- **This is a spoken-language habit more than a written one** — careful writing tends to spell out the result; conversation very often doesn't need to.

```dialog
note: B never states the result — から alone signals "that's why I can't," licensed by A's invitation.
A: 今夜[こんや]、飲[の]みに行[い]かない？ | Want to go for a drink tonight?
B: ごめん、明日[あした]早[はや]いから。 | Sorry, I've got an early start tomorrow.
```

@@ source — where something starts from

**Form:** `N + から`

- **から marks where something begins its path** — a place, a person, or a material something started as.
- **With a place, から looks back to the start; に points ahead to the goal** — 大阪から — from Osaka — and 大阪に — to Osaka — describe the same trip from opposite ends.

```eg
from: 大阪[おおさか]から来[き]た | Came from Osaka.
to: 大阪[おおさか]に行[い]く | Going to Osaka.
```

-> Contrast: [[ni#destination]] — に marking the goal a journey moves toward, から's opposite end

### から never marks the receiver

- **から only ever marks the giver, never the receiver** — 友達からもらった — got it from a friend — takes から; handing something the other way takes に, never から.
- **The material sense marks a transformation** — 米から酒を造る — sake is brewed from rice — treats the rice as changed into something else entirely.

!!! warning "English Trap"
    English "from" covers both directions of a gift or a message — "a letter from my mother", "a call from work" — which makes it tempting to reach for から when naming who something goes TO as well.

    から only ever marks the source. Giving verbs mark the recipient with に, never から: 友達にプレゼントをあげた — I gave a friend a present — not 友達からあげた.

Where the material stays recognizably itself in the result, で takes over from から — a folded paper crane is still paper, but rice brewed into sake no longer looks or behaves like rice.

-> Compare: [[kara-de#kara-de]] — から versus で for what something is made from, depending on whether the material changes

@@ temporal-since — から marking a start time

**Form:** `Time-N + から` · `から〜まで` for a bounded range

- **から marks when something starts, with no built-in endpoint** — 三時から — since three o'clock — only says where the clock started counting.
- **Pair it with まで to close the range** — から alone leaves the endpoint to context; から〜まで states both ends.
- **から attaches to a time word; てから attaches to a verb** — the starting-point idea is the same, but てから marks after doing something rather than a clock time.
- **Signs and notices lean on から constantly** — opening hours, schedules and event listings almost always state a start time this way.

```eg
午前[ごぜん]10時[じ]から営業[えいぎょう]しています。 | Open for business from 10 AM.
```

-> Compare: [[te-kara#after-doing]] — から attached after a verb's て-form instead of after a time word

Left on its own, から's endpoint is whatever the situation makes obvious — often "and it's still true now." Stating that endpoint explicitly is まで's job, and stacking the two together covers a whole range in one phrase.
