The volitional form lets a speaker lean into an action: decide “I'll do it,” invite someone with “let's,” or place that choice inside a continuing intention such as 行こうと思っている — I'm thinking of going.

Those readings share one core. The speaker presents the action as something to move toward, then context decides whose movement is involved: the speaker's own, the group's, or a plan still being considered.

Register and sentence ending matter especially in invitations. 行こう — let's go — is bare; 行こうか — shall we go — opens a proposal. 行こうよ — come on, let's go — urges, while 行きましょうか — shall we go — adds polite distance.

@@ tldr

The volitional presents an action as one to move toward: I'll do it or let's do it.
か opens a proposal, よ urges, ましょう adds polite distance, and と思っている turns the volitional choice into a current intention.

@@ main

**Form:** `Godan final -u → corresponding long -ō` · `ichidan -る → -よう` · `する → しよう` · `来る → 来よう`

| verb type — how it changes | dictionary form — meaning | volitional form — usual meaning |
|---|---|---|
| godan — final く becomes こう | 行く — go | 行こう — I'll go / let's go |
| godan — final す becomes そう | 話す — speak | 話そう — I'll speak / let's speak |
| godan — final つ becomes とう | 待つ — wait | 待とう — I'll wait / let's wait |
| ichidan — replace る with よう | 食べる — eat | 食べよう — I'll eat / let's eat |
| irregular — fixed form | する — do | しよう — I'll do it / let's do it |
| irregular — fixed form | 来る — come | 来よう — I'll come / let's come |

- **Present an action as a choice to move toward** — よし、始めよう — all right, let's begin — commits the speaker to starting and may invite others along.
- **Choose it after asking whose decision is being voiced** — in self-talk it can mean I'll do it; with a shared activity it can mean let's do it. Context, not the ending alone, supplies the participants.
- **The plain form is casual to neutral** — it is natural with oneself and close others. In a formal invitation, the ましょう family gives the relationship more room.
- **The volitional is not a future tense** — 明日行こう can concern tomorrow, but the form contributes decision or proposal, not time by itself.
- **There is no ordinary matching negative ending** — everyday Japanese does not turn 行かない into a simple negative volitional. It chooses another predicate, such as 今日は行くのをやめよう — I'll decide not to go today.

```ex
よし、今日[きょう]はここまでにしよう。
en: All right, let's stop here for today.
lit: all right, let's make it up to here today
note: The speaker turns a possible stopping point into a decision for the group.
bd: よし、|all right / 今日は|today / ここまでに|up to here / しよう*|let's make it
pat: plain volitional as a decision or shared proposal
```

### One form, two participants

```eg
self-directed: 明日[あした]、早[はや]く起[お]きよう | I'll get up early tomorrow.
shared: 明日[あした]、一緒[いっしょ]に勉強[べんきょう]しよう | Let's study together tomorrow.
```

The first speaker directs themself toward an action. 一緒に in the second makes shared action explicit, but a shared situation can license the invitation without that word.

### Choosing not to act

To decide to leave something undone, Japanese often places the volitional on another predicate. 行かないでおこう — I'll leave it at not going — uses the volitional of ておく rather than a negative volitional ending on 行く.

-> Compare: [[te-oku]] — leaving an action undone or preparing in advance with ておく

### Plain volitionals in conversation

Plain volitionals often stand alone: 帰ろう — let's go home / I'll head home. Rising intonation can turn the same form toward the listener: 帰ろう？ — shall we go home?

Fast speech especially compresses forms before か or かな. 行こうか — shall we go — becomes 行こっか — shall we go; 食べようかな — maybe I'll eat — becomes 食べよっかな — maybe I'll eat. These are spoken reductions, not new conjugations.

@@ mashou — polite decisions and invitations

**Form:** `V-ます stem + ましょう` · proposal `V-ます stem + ましょうか`

ましょう is the polite volitional. Without か, it can announce a shared course of action or guide the listener: 次を見ましょう — let's look at the next one. With か, it more explicitly offers the proposal for joint consideration.

```dialog
note: A checks whether the group is ready; B accepts and turns beginning into a polite shared decision.
A: そろそろ始[はじ]めますか。 | Shall we get started soon?
B: はい、始[はじ]めましょう。 | Yes, let's begin.
```

### Invitation distance

These forms can all invite, but they do not give the listener the same amount of room.

| form — meaning | what the speaker does — meaning | how it lands — social effect |
|---|---|---|
| 行こう？ — shall we go? | checks willingness through intonation — casual invitation | intimate and light — common with close others |
| 行こうか — shall we go? | states a proposal and asks about it — casual joint decision | casual but explicit — the speaker initiates |
| 行きましょうか — shall we go? | offers a polite shared proposal — joint decision with distance | neutral polite — suitable beyond close relationships |
| 行きませんか — would you like to go? | invites through a negative question — leaves refusal more room | polite and less presumptive — often the most deferential here |

This ladder is about how the invitation lands, not about learner difficulty. 行こう？ is not a lesser or advanced version; it is the ordinary form many learners hear first among friends.

ましょう without か can sound more directive because it presents the shared course as ready to adopt. Teachers, guides, and instructional writing use it naturally: では、例を見ましょう — now, let's look at an example.

@@ ikoukei-ka — opening a proposal

**Form:** `Volitional + か` · inward deliberation `Volitional + かな` · spoken `-おうか / -ようか → -おっか / -よっか`

In an invitation, か turns the volitional into an explicit proposal for consideration. 行こうか — shall we go — initiates a possible shared action and lets the other person respond.

```eg
intonation only: そろそろ行[い]こう？ | Shall we get going?
explicit か: そろそろ行[い]こうか | Shall we get going?
```

The first is lighter and relies on questioning intonation. The second packages the idea more clearly as the speaker's proposal. Neither difference is a fixed politeness level; voice, relationship, and situation still matter.

### Spoken compression

行こうか commonly becomes 行こっか in casual speech, and 食べようか becomes 食べよっか. The small っ reflects the compressed sound before か; careful writing normally keeps the full form.

```eg
full casual: もう帰[かえ]ろうか | Shall we head home now?
compressed speech: もう帰[かえ]ろっか | Shall we head home now?
```

When the volitional stacks on contracted ちゃう, ちゃおっか — shall we just do it — often suggests yielding to momentum already present, while plain 行こっか — shall we go — can simply initiate going.

The related page follows the compression てしまおうか — shall we just do it — to ちゃおうか — shall we just do it — to ちゃおっか — shall we just do it. Each step is more casual, and the last is limited to speech.

-> Compare: [[te-shimau#chaou]] — the ちゃおうか forms that yield to momentum already present

### Deliberating with かな

Adding な after か turns the question inward. 行こうかな — maybe I'll go / I wonder if I should go — lets the speaker consider their own next move without directly inviting an answer.

```eg
self-talk: 週末[しゅうまつ]は海[うみ]へ行[い]こうかな | Maybe I'll go to the sea this weekend.
compressed self-talk: そろそろ行[い]こっかな | Maybe I'll get going soon.
```

Another person may still respond, but the form presents the deliberation as self-directed. In quick speech, 行こっかな is casual and intimate; the full 行こうかな is neutral enough for a wider range of conversation.

@@ ikoukei-yo — encouragement and insistence

**Form:** `Volitional + よ`

In an invitation, よ presents the proposed action with extra confidence and pushes it toward the listener. 行こうよ is often warm encouragement—come on, let's go—but it can become insistence when the listener is reluctant.

```eg
open proposal: 一緒[いっしょ]に行[い]こうか | Shall we go together?
urging proposal: 一緒[いっしょ]に行[い]こうよ | Come on, let's go together.
```

か asks whether to adopt the proposal. よ presents the action as the direction the speaker wants the pair or group to take. That makes よ useful for enthusiasm and persuasion, but also riskier when the listener has already resisted.

```dialog
note: A's hesitation gives B's よ something to push against; the line is encouragement rather than a neutral question.
A: まだ行[い]くか迷[まよ]ってる。 | I'm still not sure whether to go.
B: 行[い]こうよ。きっと楽[たの]しいよ。 | Come on, let's go. It'll be fun.
```

The same stance difference appears when a volitional is built on ちゃう: ちゃおうよ — come on, let's just do it — urges, while ちゃおうか — shall we just do it — proposes. The てしまう page owns those contracted forms.

-> Contrast: [[te-shimau#chaou]] — ちゃおうか and ちゃおうよ inside the てしまう system

### Social pressure

Among close friends, 行こうよ can sound supportive or playfully eager. Said after a refusal, or by someone with institutional power, it may pressure the listener. The particle does not encode hostility; it strengthens the speaker's push toward the action.

In formal invitations, ましょうか — shall we — or 行きませんか — would you like to — usually leaves more room. In private self-talk, かな moves in the opposite direction and turns the choice inward.

@@ ikoukei-to-omotte-iru — a current intention

**Form:** `Volitional + と + 思っている` · polite `Volitional + と思っています` · conversational `Volitional + と思ってる`

This construction presents the volitional choice as a thought the speaker currently holds. 行こうと思っている naturally means I'm thinking of going or I intend to go: the decision has taken shape, but the sentence still presents it through the speaker's thinking.

```ex
来年[らいねん]、日本[にほん]で働[はたら]こうと思[おも]っています。
en: I'm thinking of working in Japan next year.
lit: I am holding the thought “I will work in Japan next year”
note: The volitional supplies the chosen direction, and 思っている presents that thought as current.
bd: 来年、|next year / 日本で|in Japan / 働こう*|I will work / と|that / 思っています*|am thinking
pat: volitional choice + と + current state of thought
reg: casual|来年、日本で働こうと思ってる。 / polite|来年、日本で働こうと思っています。
```

### The thought is ongoing

The ている belongs to 思う: it presents the intention as a thought currently maintained, not the intended action as already in progress. 来年働こうと思っている does not say the work has begun.

With the first person, it reports the speaker's own intention directly. With someone else, context needs a basis for knowing their intention: 本人は参加しようと思っているそうだ — apparently, they intend to participate.

### A leaning and a settled plan

意向形＋と思っている often sounds like a current leaning or intention taking shape around now. つもり presents the plan as more settled. This is a useful contrast, not a scale that fixes how certain every speaker must be.

-> Compare: [[tsumori]] — a settled intention

### Negating the intention

Negating 思っている denies that the intention is currently held. Negating the quoted clause produces a prediction instead, so the position of the negative matters.

```eg
intention denied: 行[い]こうと思[おも]っていない | I'm not thinking of going.
negative prediction: 行[い]かないと思[おも]っている | I think I won't go.
```

The second sentence does not create a negative volitional. Its content is plain 行かない — won't go — inside an ordinary thought.

### Current intentions in conversation and writing

Conversation commonly drops the い in 思っている: 留学しようと思ってる — I'm thinking of studying abroad. Polite speech uses 留学しようと思っています — I'm thinking of studying abroad — and careful writing normally keeps the full 思っている.

The construction can report a firm intention, especially with decisive context. Do not treat “thinking of” as a required weak translation; it is a natural English default that preserves the form's perspective.

@@ ikoukei-to-omotte-ita — an earlier intention

**Form:** `Volitional + と + 思っていた` · polite `Volitional + と思っていました` · conversational `Volitional + と思ってた`

Changing 思っている to 思っていた moves the held intention into the past. It often introduces a plan that circumstances changed, but cancellation is an inference from context, not part of the grammar.

```ex
早[はや]く帰[かえ]ろうと思[おも]っていたけど、会議[かいぎ]が長引[ながび]いた。
en: I had intended to go home early, but the meeting ran long.
lit: I had been holding the thought “I'll go home early,” but the meeting ran long
note: The second clause explains why the earlier intention was not carried out.
bd: 早く|early / 帰ろう*|I will go home / と|that / 思っていた*|had been thinking / けど、|but / 会議が|the meeting / 長引いた|ran long
pat: volitional choice + past state of thought
```

### Current and earlier plans

```eg
current: 来年[らいねん]、留学[りゅうがく]しようと思[おも]っている | I'm thinking of studying abroad next year.
earlier: 来年[らいねん]、留学[りゅうがく]しようと思[おも]っていた | I had been thinking of studying abroad next year.
```

The past form places the intention in an earlier mental state. The speaker may still intend to study abroad, may have abandoned the plan, or may be contrasting an old plan with a new one; the following context decides.

### Earlier intentions in conversation and writing

Casual speech often says 帰ろうと思ってた — I was thinking of going home, while polite speech uses 帰ろうと思っていました — I had been thinking of going home. Written narratives favor the full form and often follow it with けれど or が to explain what changed.

Negation works at the same two levels as in the present: 行こうと思っていなかった — I had not been thinking of going — denies an earlier intention, while 行かないと思っていた — I thought I wouldn't go — reports an earlier prediction.
