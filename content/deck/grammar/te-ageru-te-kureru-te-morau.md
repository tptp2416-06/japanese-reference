あげる, くれる and もらう start out as ordinary verbs of giving and receiving: 花をあげる — give flowers — もらう — receive something — くれる — someone gives something to me. Each one already fixes a direction: あげる and もらう can point in either direction depending on who the subject is, but くれる only ever points toward the speaker.

Attach any of the three to a te-form, and the same directions apply to an ACTION instead of an object. 手伝ってあげる isn't help handed over as a thing — it's the helping itself, done as a favor, in that direction.

The verb that follows て tells you what got done; the auxiliary tells you who it was done for.

That's the whole system: every sentence built this way names an action AND states who benefited from it. English can leave the beneficiary unsaid — I helped doesn't say who for. Japanese, once one of these three auxiliaries is in the sentence, cannot.

Before reaching for one, decide two things:

- **Who benefits** — you, or someone else. This choice picks あげる, くれる or もらう.
- **How much deference the moment calls for** — relevant when you're asking for the favor rather than reporting it. This climbs or drops the register from there.

@@ tldr

てあげる, てくれる and てもらう mark who an action was done FOR — the auxiliary names the beneficiary, not just the doer.
てくれる — someone did it for me. てもらう — I received the favor of someone doing it. てあげる — I did it for someone else.
Requests built on these, from てくれる？ up to ていただけないでしょうか, are one system at climbing levels of deference, not fourteen unrelated forms.

@@ te-ageru — doing something for someone else

**Form:** `V-て + あげる`

- **あげる frames the action as a favor the speaker extends** — 教えてあげる — I'll teach them — presents the teaching as something given, not just done.
- **It fits peers and juniors, and can sound patronizing higher up** — offering to help a boss or a teacher with てあげる can come across as generosity from a position of ability, aimed down rather than across.
- **お手伝いしましょうか — shall I help you? — is the safer offer toward someone senior**, built without あげる at all.
- **Nothing about あげる implies the favor was asked for** — 弟にお菓子を買ってあげた — I bought my little brother some sweets — can be entirely unprompted generosity.

-> Compare: [[ikoukei#mashou]] — the volitional offer this page's superior-register alternative is built on

```eg
持ってあげる。 | I'll carry it for you.
```

@@ te-agetara — suggesting the favor to someone else

**Form:** `V-て + あげたら`

- **あげたら doesn't offer a favor — it suggests that someone else offer one.** 引っ越すの、手伝ってあげたら？ — why don't you help them move? — proposes that the listener become the giver, not the speaker.
- **The rising, unfinished たら carries the suggestion on its own** — no どう or いい is required, though either can follow to make the suggestion more explicit: 手伝ってあげたらどう？ — why don't you help them?

```eg
連絡してあげたら？ | Why don't you get in touch with them?
```

@@ te-kureru — someone does it for me

**Form:** `V-て + くれる`

- **くれる is the emotional center of this whole system** — it doesn't just report that something happened; it reports that it happened FOR the speaker, as a gift received rather than a fact observed.
- **The subject is the giver, not the speaker** — 友達が迎えに来てくれた — a friend came to pick me up — puts the friend in the が role; the speaker, as beneficiary, often isn't named at all.
- **Even ordinary actions carry gratitude once くれる attaches** — 思い出させてくれた — you reminded me [and I'm grateful for it] — turns a neutral action into a received kindness.

```ex
友達[ともだち]が空港[くうこう]まで迎[むか]えに来[き]てくれた。
en: A friend came all the way to the airport to pick me up.
note: The friend is the sentence's subject; the speaker never needs to appear as the one who benefited — くれる already says so.
bd: 友達が|a friend / 空港まで|all the way to the airport / 迎えに来てくれた*|came to pick me up
pat: giver が + V-てくれる, speaker as unstated beneficiary
```

@@ te-kureru-q — asking a friend directly

**Form:** `V-て + くれる？`

The friend register of the request ladder this page's forms climb together — the bottom rung, not a lesser version of the polite ones above it.

- **くれる？ asks a peer for a favor with no cushioning at all** — 手伝ってくれる？ — will you help me? — relies entirely on the relationship, not on the grammar, to keep it polite.
- **It's the form closest to ordinary spoken requests** — this is what gets said between friends and family constantly, while てくれますか and the もらう/いただく forms below handle distance.

```dialog
note: A trusts the closeness of the relationship to carry a request with no polite cushioning at all.
A: もうちょっと ゆっくり話[はな]してくれる？ | Could you talk a bit slower?
B: あ、ごめん。 | Oh, sorry.
```

- **くれる can attach to a sustained ている state instead of a single action, by way of ていて** — 待っていてくれる？ — will you keep waiting for me? — asks the listener to maintain a state across a stretch of time, not just perform one action once.
- **The extra ていて layer is what carries the duration** — 待ってくれる？ asks for the act of waiting; 待っていてくれる？ asks for that waiting to be sustained, often paired with a condition like でも that makes the duration explicit.

-> Prerequisite: [[te-iru]] — the sustained state くれる attaches to here

```eg
one action: 待[ま]ってくれる？* | Will you wait for me?
sustained state: 待[ま]っていてくれる？* | Will you keep waiting for me?
```

@@ te-kurenai — noting that the favor didn't happen

**Form:** `V-て + くれない` (statement)

- **As a plain statement, てくれない reports an absence, often with some frustration behind it** — 全然手伝ってくれない — they never help me [at all] — names a favor that keeps not arriving.
- **Nothing here is a request** — the sentence describes a pattern, not something being asked for right now.

!!! note "What's in scope"
    てくれない as a statement and てくれない？ as a question look identical on the page. Only rising intonation, a question mark, or context marks the difference — the grammar itself doesn't distinguish a flat report that the favor isn't coming from a request asking for it.

@@ te-kurenai-q — a softer casual request

**Form:** `V-て + くれない？`

- **Asking くれる in the negative softens the request within casual register** — 手伝ってくれない？ offers a gentler way to decline than plain 手伝ってくれる？; もらえない？ and いただけないでしょうか — could you possibly do that for me — lean on the same softening idea at higher registers, there through the negative of a potential form (もらえる, いただける) rather than plain くれる.
- **Still entirely casual** — the softening comes from asking in the negative, not from any added politeness marker.

```eg
casual direct: 手伝[てつだ]ってくれる？* | Will you help me?
casual softened: 手伝[てつだ]ってくれない？* | Won't you help me?
```

@@ te-kudasaru — the honorific auxiliary

**Form:** `V-て + くださる`

- **くださる is くれる raised to describe a respected person's own action** — 先生が案内してくださった — the teacher kindly showed me around — elevates the giver, not the favor itself.
- **It's mainly used to talk ABOUT someone's kindness, not to ask for it directly** — the request built on this same politeness level is ていただけないでしょうか — could you possibly do that for me — further down this page, built from いただく rather than くださる.

```eg
道[みち]に迷[まよ]っている時[とき]、知[し]らない人[ひと]が案内[あんない]してくださった。 | While I was lost, a stranger was kind enough to show me the way.
```

@@ te-morau — receiving the favor, reversed

**Form:** `V-て + もらう`

もらう keeps the same favor, but tells it from the other side.

- **The geometry reverses** — てくれる puts the giver in the subject role; てもらう puts the receiver there instead, marking the giver with に. The same event can be described from either side, as the pair below shows.
- **もらう structurally asks whether the speaker may receive the action from the listener, which is why it reads as humbler than plain くれる requests** — even before any keigo is added, the receiving frame itself does some of the softening.

```eg
giver as subject: 友達[ともだち]が手伝[てつだ]ってくれた。* | A friend helped me.
receiver as subject: 友達[ともだち]に手伝[てつだ]ってもらった。* | I had a friend help me. / A friend helped me, and I'm the one who asked.
```

@@ te-moraenai-q — asking casually, softened

**Form:** `V-て + もらえない？`

- **もらえない？ is the receiving frame's version of the negative-potential softening** — 手伝ってもらえない？ — could I get you to help me? — asks from the receiver's side, with a gentle decline already built in.
- **A trailing reason is common, and it's normal for it to land after the request rather than before it** — 一人じゃ無理だから, added on, explains the ask instead of justifying it in advance.

```dialog
note: The reason for the request trails the ask itself, softening it further after the fact.
A: ねえ、これ手伝[てつだ]ってもらえない？一人[ひとり]じゃ無理[むり]だから。 | Hey, could you help me with this? I can't do it alone.
B: いいよ。 | Sure.
```

@@ te-moraemasu-ka — asking politely

**Form:** `V-て + もらえますか`

- **もらえますか is the standard polite step up from もらえない？** — 撮ってもらえますか — could you take a photo for me? — safe with strangers and acquaintances, without reaching for いただく's extra humility.

```eg
教[おし]えてもらえますか。 | Could you teach me?
```

@@ te-itadaku — the humble receiving auxiliary

**Form:** `V-て + いただく`

- **いただく is もらう lowered into 謙譲語** — the receiving geometry is unchanged; only the speaker's own position drops, which is what elevates the giver by comparison.
- **It reports a favor received, the same way もらう does, just at a more deferential register** — 先生に教えていただいた — I had my teacher teach me — describes the same event as 教えてもらった — I had someone teach me — just spoken more humbly.

```eg
先生[せんせい]に日本語[にほんご]を教[おし]えていただいた。 | I was taught Japanese by my teacher.
```

@@ te-itadakemasu-ka — a polite humble request

**Form:** `V-て + いただけますか`

- **いただけますか asks directly whether the favor is available, from the humble side** — 撮っていただけますか — could you take a photo for me? — sits above もらえますか in deference without yet adding the negative-potential cushion below.

```eg
確認[かくにん]していただけますか。 | Could I ask you to check this?
```

@@ te-itadakemasen-ka — a humbler, softened request

**Form:** `V-て + いただけませんか`

- **いただけませんか adds the same easy-no cushion もらえない？ uses, at the humble register** — 手伝っていただけませんか — could you possibly help me — offers a graceful way to decline while still asking directly.

```eg
少[すこ]しお待[ま]ちいただけませんか。 | Could you wait just a moment?
```

@@ te-itadakenai-deshou-ka — the top rung

**Form:** `V-て + いただけないでしょうか`

The most deferential form this system reaches — doubly cushioned, for an imposition the speaker already knows is inconvenient.

- **Two softeners stack** — the negative potential いただけない asks without demanding, and でしょうか turns the whole thing into a conjecture rather than a direct question.
- **It's common to open with an apology before the request even arrives** — 申し訳ありませんが precedes it more often than not, doing the work an explicit reason would otherwise do.

```ex
申[もう]し訳[わけ]ありませんが、開始[かいし]時間[じかん]を一時間[いちじかん]ほど遅[おく]らせていただけないでしょうか。
en: I'm terribly sorry, but could we possibly push the start time back by about an hour?
note: No reason is given for the delay — the apology and the doubled softening carry the weight a justification would otherwise supply.
bd: 申し訳ありませんが、|I'm terribly sorry, but / 開始時間を|the start time / 一時間ほど|by about an hour / 遅らせていただけないでしょうか*|could we possibly push back
pat: V-causative + ていただけないでしょうか, maximum deference
```

```dialog
note: The speaker already expects the request to be an imposition, and the apology carries as much of the weight as the grammar does.
A: 申[もう]し訳[わけ]ありませんが、明日[あした]までに資料[しりょう]を送[おく]っていただけないでしょうか。 | I'm terribly sorry, but could you possibly send the materials by tomorrow?
B: 承知[しょうち]しました。 | Understood.
```

### Climbing the ladder together

Line the whole family up, and the movement from friend to stranger to imposed-upon stranger becomes visible in one table.

| form | frame | how it lands |
|---|---|---|
| ていただけないでしょうか — could you possibly...? | humble receiving, doubly cushioned | most deferential; apologizing for an imposition, business and service contexts |
| ていただけませんか — could you possibly...? | humble receiving, softened | deferential; common in polite workplace and service requests |
| ていただけますか — could you...? | humble receiving | polite and direct; a shade less cushioned than ませんか |
| てもらえますか — could you...? | plain receiving | standard polite; safe with strangers and acquaintances |
| てもらえない？ — could you...? | plain receiving, casual | casual, softened; close acquaintances |
| てくれない？ — won't you...? | plain giving, casual | casual, softened; friends and family |
| てくれる？ — will you...? | plain giving, casual | very common in conversation; friends and family, no cushioning needed |

None of these seven forms is a weaker version of another — each is what a speaker actually reaches for at its own social distance. The casual bottom rung isn't a shortcut around politeness; it's the form the relationship calls for.

!!! warning "English Trap"
    てもらう often comes out in English translation sounding passive — having something done to you, getting someone to do something — and that resemblance pulls learners toward Japanese's actual passive, られる, for the same idea. The two are not interchangeable.

    てもらう reports a favor the speaker asked for or benefited from. られる can report something that happened TO the speaker, often unwelcome, with no request or benefit involved.

    ```eg
    favor: 髪[かみ]を切[き]ってもらった。* | I had my hair cut [and arranged it, and it helped me].
    adversity: 電車[でんしゃ]で足[あし]を踏[ふ]まれた。* | Someone stepped on my foot on the train.
    ```

    Both can mark the other party with に, which is exactly what invites the confusion — but one names a favor, and the other doesn't.

-> Contrast: [[rareru#adversity-passive]] — an event that happens to the speaker, often unwelcome, with no benefit implied

### In conversation

Fragments carry the whole request once context supplies the rest:

- 手伝って？ — Help? [reads as a favor for the speaker, the sense くれる would add]
- お願いできますか？ — Could I ask you? [without naming the favor at all]
- いいよ。 — Sure. [accepting any of the forms above]

くれない alone, with no question mark and falling intonation, reads as the plain statement noting an absence, not a request — the fragment keeps whichever reading its intonation gives it in speech.
