The そう family is one machine: read the evidence in front of you and say what
it looks like. The senses differ in what the evidence points at — a property
(appearance) or an event on the verge of happening (imminence) — and in how
the negative is built on each side. One near-miss to keep out of this page:
hearsay そうだ, which attaches to a whole plain clause (雨が降るそうだ — "I
hear it'll rain") and reports what you were told, not what you see.

@@ appearance

Adjective stem + そう: 美味しそう looks tasty, 元気そう seems well. The
judgment comes from what is visible right now, and it stays a judgment — you
haven't tasted it yet, which is exactly what the form signals. いい is
irregular: よさそう.

@@ imminence

Verb stem + そう: 落ちそう it's about to fall, 泣きそう I'm about to cry.
Same shape as the appearance sense, but the evidence points at an event
about to happen rather than a property. Which reading you get follows from
what そう attached to — an adjective gives appearance, a verb gives
imminence.

@@ そうにない

The verb-side negative: 終わりそうにない — it doesn't look like it's going to
end. Not the mirror of なさそう: this one denies an event's likelihood, and it
carries a fairly strong "no sign of it at all".

@@ そうになる

Came to the brink: 転びそうになった — I nearly fell. The event reached
"about to happen" and, implicitly, didn't. Almost always in the past tense
for exactly that reason.

@@ そうに見える

Wraps the impression in an explicit "looks": 高そうに見える — it looks
expensive. Plain そうだ already means "seems", so adding に見える foregrounds
that the impression is specifically visual — often with a faint hedge that
looks might deceive.

@@ なさそう

The adjective-side negative: 美味しくなさそう doesn't look tasty, 問題なさそう
looks like no problem. Built on ない, so it pairs with the appearance sense —
compare そうにない above for denying that an event looks likely.
