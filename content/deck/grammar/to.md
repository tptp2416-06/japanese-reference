と has two lives that grow from the same basic idea: it joins one thing to another. With nouns, it joins people or items. With clauses, it joins two events so tightly that the second is presented as the regular or automatic result of the first.

That tight connection is the key to choosing conditional と. Use it when you want the listener to treat the result as built into the condition: a machine responds, a season brings a change, or one repeated action reliably leads to another.

This is also why と is a poor default for every English “if.” In its ordinary condition-result use, it does not simply imagine a possibility; it presents what follows as dependable.

@@ tldr

Conditional と presents the second event as the regular, automatic, or readily predictable result of the first.
With nouns, と instead joins listed items or marks the person you do something with. Context tells these two lives apart.

@@ conditional — automatic results

**Form:** `V-plain + と` · `i-adjective + と` · `N / na-adjective + だと`

- **Present the link as dependable** — このボタンを押すと、画面が明るくなる — when you press this button, the screen gets brighter — treats the response as part of how the button works.
- **Choose it after asking what reliably follows** — if your point is that the same condition normally produces the same result, と lets you present that connection without reopening it for negotiation.
- **Time usually moves forward** — the condition is met first, and the consequence follows. The two clauses are not merely two unrelated facts joined by “and.”
- **The form itself is register-neutral** — it works in conversation, instructions, explanations, and formal writing. Politeness appears elsewhere in the sentence: 押すと分かります — when you press it, you will understand.
- **Negation keeps the same logic** — 急がないと間に合わない — if we don't hurry, we won't make it — presents missing the deadline as the expected consequence of not hurrying.

```ex
このボタンを押[お]すと、画面[がめん]が明[あか]るくなる。
en: When you press this button, the screen gets brighter.
note: The speaker presents the response as part of the device's regular operation, not as a one-time guess.
bd: このボタンを|this button / 押すと、*|when you press / 画面が|the screen / 明るくなる|gets brighter
pat: condition + と, dependable consequence
```

The surrounding question often makes that dependable-result reading useful:

```dialog
note: A asks how the control works, so B gives the button's regular result.
A: このボタン、何[なに]？ | What does this button do?
B: 押[お]すと、画面[がめん]が明[あか]るくなるよ。 | When you press it, the screen gets brighter.
```

### Consequences outside the speaker's control

In the ordinary predictive use described here, the consequence is presented as something that follows rather than something the speaker decides on the spot. That makes commands, requests, invitations, and personal intentions a poor fit in the result clause.

```eg
regular result: 春[はる]になると、桜[さくら]が咲[さ]く | When spring comes, the cherry trees bloom.
speaker's plan: 春[はる]になったら、京都[きょうと]へ行[い]こう | When spring comes, let's go to Kyoto.
```

The second sentence uses たら because going is a proposal, not an automatic consequence of spring arriving.

This is a strong production default, not a claim that every sentence containing human action is impossible with と. Habits and procedures can still be presented as regular outcomes.

-> Compare: [[tara#conditional]] — the four conditionals side by side

なら is useful when the condition picks up a situation someone has mentioned and the response is advice or evaluation, rather than an automatic next event.

-> Compare: [[nara]] — a condition that reacts to the situation under discussion

!!! warning "English Trap"
    English “if” does not tell you which Japanese conditional to choose. Conditional と adds a specific commitment: within the speaker's framing, the consequence follows regularly or predictably.

    If the result is a request, decision, invitation, or one-time plan, do not translate “if” mechanically with と. First decide what relationship you want the two clauses to have.

### Negative conditions in conversation

Speech often leaves off the consequence when it is obvious. もう行かないと literally opens — if I don't go now… — with the bad result understood, it naturally means I have to go now or I'd better go.

```eg
もう寝[ね]ないと | I have to get to bed.
急[いそ]がないと | We have to hurry.
```

This shortened use does not turn と itself into an obligation marker. The pressure comes from the unfavorable consequence the listener supplies from context.

@@ natural-consequence — regular and natural consequences

**Form:** `Condition in plain form + と + regular / natural consequence`

Use this pattern for relationships the speaker treats as repeatable: natural changes, operating instructions, habits, routes, and cause-and-effect explanations. The result need not be a scientific law; it only needs to be presented as stable in the situation being described.

```eg
季節[きせつ]: 秋[あき]になると、日[ひ]が短[みじか]くなる | When autumn comes, the days get shorter.
習慣[しゅうかん]: この音楽[おんがく]を聞[き]くと、よく眠[ねむ]れる | When I listen to this music, I sleep well.
道順[みちじゅん]: 次[つぎ]の角[かど]を曲[ま]がると、駅[えき]が見[み]える | When you turn at the next corner, you can see the station.
```

The speaker may be generalizing from personal experience rather than stating an exceptionless fact. この音楽を聞くと、よく眠れる describes a dependable tendency for this speaker; it does not claim that the music guarantees sleep every time.

Instructions and public information often favor と because they describe what a control or route does: カードを入れると、ドアが開きます — when you insert the card, the door opens. The polite ending changes how the explanation addresses the reader, not the conditional relationship.

### Repeated past results

The consequence can be past when the speaker is describing a former routine. 子供のころ、夏になると海へ行った — when summer came, we would go to the sea — presents the trip as a regular result within that past period.

```eg
子供[こども]のころ、この店[みせ]に来[く]ると、いつもアイスを買[か]った | When I came to this shop as a child, I always bought ice cream.
```

Here the human action is natural because the entire sentence describes a habit. The restriction on speaker-controlled consequences concerns an on-the-spot command, request, or decision, not every action performed by a person.

### A nearby conditional

ば can also present a condition as sufficient for a result. Conditional と more strongly invites the listener to see an event sequence or dependable response; ば directs attention to the condition that would make the result hold.

-> Compare: [[ba]] — conditions presented as sufficient or decisive

@@ discovery — what regularly comes into view

**Form:** `Action in plain form + と + discovered state / scene`

In its discovery use, と presents what comes into view after an action:

- **Opening something** — reveals a scene.
- **Turning a corner** — reveals a landmark.
- **Checking repeatedly** — produces the same finding.

The action still comes first, but the second clause is not something the subject intentionally causes.

```ex
朝[あさ]、カーテンを開[あ]けると、いつも山[やま]が見[み]えた。
en: In the morning, whenever I opened the curtains, I could always see the mountains.
note: The mountains are a finding after the action, and いつも makes the repeated pattern explicit.
bd: 朝、|in the morning / カーテンを|the curtains / 開けると、*|whenever I opened / いつも|always / 山が|the mountains / 見えた|could be seen
pat: action + と, repeated discovery
```

For this page's core distinction, と narrates a general or repeated discovery pattern, while たら naturally foregrounds a one-time finding. The two can overlap in narrative contexts, but they guide attention differently: と toward the recurring sequence, たら toward what happened on that occasion.

-> Compare: [[tara#discovery]] — a one-time finding after the condition is met

The discovered scene can be surprising, but surprise is not built into と. It comes from the content and context: 箱を開けると、中はいつも空だった — whenever I opened the box, it was always empty inside — can be a neutral report or an unwelcome finding.

@@ da-to — conditions with nouns and な-adjectives

**Form:** `N + だと` · `na-adjective stem + だと` · negative `N / na-adjective + じゃないと`

Nouns and な-adjectives need だ before conditional と. このままだと間に合わない — at this rate, we won't make it — takes the current situation as the condition and presents lateness as its expected result.

```eg
noun: 平日[へいじつ]だと、道[みち]が混[こ]む | On weekdays, the roads get crowded.
な-adjective: 静[しず]かだと、仕事[しごと]に集中[しゅうちゅう]できる | When it is quiet, I can concentrate on work.
negative: 静[しず]かじゃないと、集中[しゅうちゅう]できない | If it isn't quiet, I can't concentrate.
```

The negative often makes a necessary condition salient: 静かじゃないと集中できない says concentration will not happen unless quiet is present. That interpretation comes from the negative result and the logic of the sentence, not from a separate meaning of と.

In careful or formal writing, でないと may replace conversational じゃないと: 本人でないと受け取れません — unless you are the named person, you cannot receive it. The conditional relationship is unchanged; only the expression of the negative copula becomes more formal.

!!! note "Conditional だと and quotation"
    The だ in このままだと belongs to a noun or な-adjective predicate before conditional と. Japanese also has a separate quote-marking と in expressions built around saying or thinking.

    Those roles can look identical at the boundary, so identify what follows: a predictable consequence supports the conditional reading, while a speech or thought predicate supports the quotation reading.

-> Contrast: [[tte#quote]] — the casual quote marker, a separate role from conditional と

@@ listing — joining named items

**Form:** `N と N` · `N と N と ...`

Between nouns, と joins explicitly named members: コーヒーと紅茶 — coffee and tea. In a request such as コーヒーと紅茶をください — coffee and tea, please — both items belong to the requested list.

```eg
パンと卵[たまご]を買[か]った | I bought bread and eggs.
月曜日[げつようび]と水曜日[すいようび]に授業[じゅぎょう]がある | I have classes on Monday and Wednesday.
```

This listing use is concrete and does not create a condition. It names the members the speaker is joining for the current sentence; it does not, by itself, make a claim about every other item that may exist in the world.

The same と may appear after each item in an emphatic or contrastive list, but repeating it is not required in an ordinary two-item phrase. The particle is register-neutral and remains common in both speech and writing.

### Short lists in conversation

If the second item is already obvious, a speaker may leave the shared noun or predicate unstated, but と cannot normally stand as a complete list without context. A short reply such as パンと卵 — bread and eggs — works because the question supplies what is being bought.

```dialog
note: The question supplies the omitted predicate, so B can answer with the joined items alone.
A: 朝[あさ]ご飯[はん]、何[なに]を買[か]う？ | What should we buy for breakfast?
B: パンと卵[たまご]。 | Bread and eggs.
```

@@ accompaniment — doing something with someone

**Form:** `Person / group + と + action` · `Person / group + と一緒に + action`

After a person or group, と presents that participant as the companion or counterpart in an action. 友達と映画を見た — I watched a film with a friend — describes a shared activity, while 先生と話した — I spoke with the teacher — presents the teacher as the other participant in the conversation.

```eg
姉[あね]と旅行[りょこう]した | I traveled with my older sister.
同僚[どうりょう]と相談[そうだん]した | I discussed it with a coworker.
```

With naturally mutual actions such as 話す — talk — both participants take part in the event. With an action such as 映画を見る — watch a film — と simply says the person accompanied you; it does not imply that each person acted on the other.

一緒に can make the shared nature explicit: 家族と一緒に住んでいる — I live together with my family. It adds emphasis but is not required for the accompaniment reading.

### Companions in conversation

The rest of the sentence may be omitted when the activity is understood. 誰と？ — with whom — or 友達と — with a friend — are natural short turns. The particle survives because it carries the relationship the question is asking about.
