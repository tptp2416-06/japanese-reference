Two people are looking at the same photograph and one of them says この写真、よく撮れてるね — this photo came out well, didn't it.

The other person is looking at the same photo and can judge it perfectly well themselves. That is the condition ね is built for: the remark is handed over to be confirmed rather than delivered as news.

Say the same thing down the phone to somebody who has not seen the photo and ね is the wrong particle. What that situation wants is よ, which presents the judgement as the speaker's to supply.

ね's second job looks unrelated and is the same move applied where the listener has no access at all.

先に出るね — I'm heading out, okay? — announces a plan nobody else can confirm, and asks the listener to be alright with it rather than to agree with it.

Before adding ね, ask whether the listener can already tell. If they can, ね invites them to say so; if they cannot, ね asks them to accept what you are about to do.

Those two jobs are ね's core work, and most sentences with ね are doing one or the other. ね also drifts into looser use that is neither, exactly — keeping the listener oriented to what's happening rather than asking them to confirm or accept anything specific — and the in-conversation section below names a case. Confirm-and-accept is the strong default, not the whole of ね.

One warning about what a page cannot show. Much of ね's work is carried by the contour, and the mapping below is a tendency rather than a fixed rule: a rising ね usually asks, a falling ね usually shares. Both are written identically, so the sections below say which one they mean wherever the difference matters.

@@ tldr

ね's core job is handing a statement to the listener for confirmation, on things they can already judge for themselves.
On a plan only the speaker knows about, it does the same by asking the listener to accept the announcement rather than to agree with it.
よね combines the two: the belief is the speaker's, and the confirming is the listener's to do.

@@ agreement — inviting the listener to agree

**Form:** `plain sentence + ね` · `N / な-adj + だね` · `です・ます + ね`

- **ね presents the statement as one the listener is in a position to confirm** — 駅、混んでるね — the station's crowded, isn't it — works because both of them are standing in it.
- **A rising ね usually asks and a falling ね usually shares — a strong tendency, not a fixed switch.** Rising 〜ね？ typically requests a confirmation the speaker is not certain of; falling ね typically treats the agreement as already in place. Even shared information can carry a rise, when the speaker is pressing rather than assuming.
- **ね carries no politeness of its own.** It follows です and ます as readily as a plain form: 上手ですね — you're good at that, aren't you — is polite because of です, not because of ね.
- **Sentence-final ね takes the whole sentence in its scope.** It comments on how the statement is being delivered, not on who it is said to — strip it off and the same claim can still be said directly to the listener; what disappears is the invitation to confirm it, not the addressee.
- **Negation changes what is being agreed to, not what ね does** — このパン、あんまり甘くないね — this bread isn't very sweet, is it — is the same invitation over a negative predicate.

```ex
この店[みせ]、思[おも]ったより広[ひろ]いね。
en: This place is bigger than I expected, isn't it.
lit: this shop, more spacious than I thought, right
note: Both speakers are standing in the shop, so its size is something the
  listener can check by looking. That access is what ね trades on — the remark
  is offered for confirmation rather than delivered as news.
bd: この店、|this place, / 思ったより|than I expected / 広い|is spacious / ね*|isn't it
pat: statement + ね, offered to a listener who can see it too
reg: casual|この店、思ったより広いね。 / polite|この店、思ったより広いですね。
```

```dialog
note: what licenses ね — the quiet is available to both of them, so A offers the remark for B to confirm rather than reporting it.
A: この道[みち]、静[しず]かだね。 | This street's quiet, isn't it.
B: うん、昼間[ひるま]はいつもこんな感[かん]じ。 | Yeah, it's always like this in the daytime.
```

### The split between ね and よ

The choice between ね and よ is not about politeness, and not about emphasis. It is about who the sentence treats as already holding the information.

```eg
shared: このケーキ、甘[あま]いね | This cake's sweet, isn't it.
news: このケーキ、甘[あま]いよ | This cake's sweet, just so you know.
```

Both sentences say the cake is sweet. The first takes the listener to have tasted it too and asks them to agree; the second takes them not to have, and hands them the fact.

Get the assumption wrong and the sentence still parses, but it makes a claim about the listener. ね on something they could not know asks them to confirm what they do not have; よ on something they plainly do know can land as a correction.

Treat that as a strong tendency rather than a rule, because speakers work against it deliberately. ね on genuinely new information is the soft assertion in the next section, and よ on shared information is what makes a reminder sound pointed.

### In conversation

- **ね is not confined to the end of the sentence.** あのね、昨日、駅で… — so, yesterday, at the station… — breaks a turn into pieces and checks after each one that the listener is still following.
- **That mid-sentence ね does not disappear in careful speech; it changes shape.** あのね — um, listen — becomes あのですね — well, if I may — in formal explanation, where the plain form would be too familiar.
- **Whole turns are made of it** — そうだね — yeah, true — and だよね — right — are complete replies.
- **Lengthening changes the weight.** A drawn-out ねー reads as warmer, or as more thoroughly agreed, than a clipped ね.
- **そうですね before an answer is often just a pause, not agreement.** そうですね — well, let me see — gives the speaker a moment to think, without agreeing with anything in particular.
- **Not every ね is asking for something back.** ここに置きますね — here, I'll set this down — said while doing exactly that keeps the listener oriented to what's happening, rather than requesting their agreement or their acceptance of it.

In writing, ね appears where the writing is really speech: messages, dialogue, copy addressed to a reader. Notices and formal prose leave it out.

!!! warning "Pitfall"
    ね is not a free-floating politeness marker. In its two core jobs it hands the sentence over to the listener — for confirmation where they can judge, for acceptance where they cannot — and it is about them either way.

    Said as a self-introduction, 田中ですね hands the speaker's own name across, and with no subject pronounced the listener takes it as being about them: you're Tanaka, aren't you. 田中です — I'm Tanaka — is what introduces you.

    The instinct behind the mistake is sound, and the form that carries it is the soft assertion below, which attaches to what the speaker is about to do rather than to who they are.

@@ soft-assertion — softening your own announcement

**Form:** `a sentence about the speaker's own action + ね`, plain or polite

- **The listener cannot confirm your plans, so ね is not asking them to.** 先に出るね asks them to receive the plan and be alright with it.
- **This is the ordinary way to announce a small unilateral act** — 鍵、机の上に置いとくね — I'll leave the key on the desk.
- **The particle is doing the whole difference.** 先に出る — I'm heading out — states the plan, 先に出るよ notifies the listener of it, and 先に出るね checks that they are fine with it.
- **It attaches to requests as easily as to plans** — 忘れないでね — don't forget, will you — asks for the same acceptance about an action that is the listener's.
- **Polite speech takes it unchanged.** お先に失礼しますね — I'll be off, if that's alright — is ordinary in an office, and the politeness comes from します.

```eg
checking it is alright: 先[さき]に出[で]るね | I'm heading out, okay?
simply notifying: 先[さき]に出[で]るよ | I'm heading out.
```

Neither is impolite. ね leaves the listener something to respond to; よ asks for nothing back.

```dialog
note: what licenses ね — B's own plan is not something A can confirm, so ね asks A to take it on board rather than to agree with it.
A: 荷物[にもつ]、まだある？ | Is there anything left to carry?
B: あと一[ひと]つ。先[さき]に運[はこ]んどくね。 | One more. I'll take it ahead.
```

-> Compare: [[te-ne]] — てね, the request frame this ね sits on when the action is the listener's

### Where the two senses meet

Both senses hand the sentence to the listener. What changes is what the listener is being asked for: agreement, when they can judge for themselves, and acceptance, when they cannot.

That is also why the boundary between them is soft. 明日、少し遅れるね — I'll be a bit late tomorrow — asks the listener to accept a plan; said to someone who had already guessed, the same sentence slides toward agreement.

@@ yo-ne — checking something you think you both know

**Form:** `plain sentence + よね` · `N / な-adj + だよね` · `です・ます + よね`

- **よ presents the belief as the speaker's, ね hands it over to be confirmed.** The combination fits a recollection you are fairly sure of and would like ratified.
- **It leaves room to be wrong.** A bare ね assumes the listener is already with you; よね asks whether they are, which is why it suits half-certain memory.
- **It is very common on shared facts and arrangements** — ここ、前に来たことあるよね — we've been here before, haven't we.
- **After a noun it usually takes だ** — 明日、休みだよね — tomorrow's off, right. As with plain ね, marked feminine-coded speech can drop it: そうよね — that's right, isn't it — beside the ordinary そうだよね — that's right, isn't it.
- **Rising is the default** when it is a genuine check. A falling よね reads closer to thinking aloud.

```ex
あの鍵[かぎ]、返[かえ]したよね？
en: I gave that key back, didn't I.
lit: that key, I returned it, right
note: The speaker has their own memory of returning it and is not asking to be
  informed. よ presents that memory as theirs and ね hands it over to be
  checked, which is why よね fits a half-sure recollection where a bare ね
  would claim the listener already agreed.
bd: あの鍵、|that key, / 返した|I gave back / よね*|didn't I
pat: statement + よね, a belief put up for confirmation
```

```eg
seeking agreement: この道[みち]で合[あ]ってるね | We're on the right road, aren't we.
supplying it: この道[みち]で合[あ]ってるよ | We're on the right road, trust me.
checking a belief: この道[みち]で合[あ]ってるよね | We're on the right road, right?
```

The first takes the listener to think so already. The second tells them. The third says the speaker thinks so and would like it checked, which is the one for navigating when you are only fairly sure.

### Four ways to ask for confirmation

The rows run from the form a textbook teaches first to the most colloquial. The first three ask the listener to confirm something, and what separates them is how much agreement each one assumes it already has; the last, っけ, can ask the same way, or can be muttered with nobody listening at all, digging for a memory rather than checking one.

| how you ask | what you are doing | how it lands |
|---|---|---|
| 〜ね？ — isn't it? | offers a remark for the listener to confirm | neutral; expects their agreement, while still asking rather than assuming |
| 〜よね？ — right? | puts your own belief up for confirmation | very common in conversation; leaves room to be corrected |
| [[desho-q]] — right? | invites the listener to confirm what you both expect to be true | でしょう？ is neutral to polite and a genuine check; でしょ？ is casual and can sound like pressing them to admit it |
| [[kke#memory-retrieval]] — what was it again? | concedes you've half-forgotten — to a listener, or to yourself | casual; the only one that admits you do not know |

-> Compare: [[n-da#n-da-yo-ne]] — んだよね, the same pair stacked on んだ's explanatory frame
-> Compare: [[jan]] — じゃん, a blunter way of putting something up as obvious

!!! warning "English Trap"
    English tag questions — isn't it, right, don't you think — are the nearest equivalent, and they are far rarer than ね.

    A speaker who ends most sentences with one sounds unsure or pushy in English. In Japanese it is ordinary conversational maintenance.

    So do not read insecurity into a run of ね, and do not translate every one. English often carries the same work in the intonation and adds no word at all.

??? note "な, なあ, and the ね that is addressed to nobody"
    Sentence-final な can do a ね-like job in casual speech and often reads as more masculine, or as more inward. Lengthened なあ turns the remark inward altogether: it registers a feeling rather than asking anyone to share it.

    Dropping だ before ね — いい天気ね — lovely weather, isn't it — is the same markedly feminine move that 明日、休みよ — tomorrow's a day off, you know — makes with よ, and not simply a casual contraction.

    A different な entirely follows the dictionary form and forbids the action — 見るな — don't look. Same kana, unrelated job.
