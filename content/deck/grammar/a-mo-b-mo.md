One も adds an item to a set. Put も on each of two named items and both are
explicitly included: 犬も猫も好き "I like both dogs and cats".

The doubled marking gives the two items equal footing. Compared with a plain と
list, it often carries a small "this one too, and that one too" emphasis.

@@ tldr

`A も B も + affirmative` includes both A and B. With a negative predicate, the
same frame excludes both and is normally translated "neither A nor B".

@@ main

**Form:** `A も B も + predicate` · `A + particle + も, B + particle + も`

- **Affirmative predicates include both** — 犬も猫も好き "I like both dogs and
  cats" presents each animal as a member of the liked set.
- **Negative predicates exclude both** — 肉も魚も食べない "I eat neither meat
  nor fish" applies the negation to each listed item.
- **The two items stand as equals** — 犬と猫が好き "I like dogs and cats" is a
  plain list; 犬も猫も好き "I like both dogs and cats" highlights that each one
  is included. How emphatic it feels depends on context and delivery.
- **Case marking follows the ordinary も mechanics** — も replaces が or を on
  the listed items, while other case particles remain before it:
  東京にも大阪にも行った "I went to both Tokyo and Osaka".

```ex
犬[いぬ]も猫[ねこ]も好[す]きだ。
en: I like both dogs and cats.
bd: 犬も*|dogs too / 猫も*|cats too / 好きだ|like
pat: A も B も + affirmative predicate
```

### Why does the same frame mean "neither"?

The two も markers still include both items in the set under discussion. The
negative predicate then denies the statement for every listed member:
コーヒーも紅茶も飲まない "I drink neither coffee nor tea".

Nothing inside も itself means "neither". That reading comes from doubled も
together with negation.
