A Japanese sentence often works in two layers. One element is lifted out and set
at the front as the frame — this is what we are talking about now — and
everything after it is read inside that frame until something replaces it.

は is the particle that does the lifting. It is written は and pronounced "wa"
when it is doing this job, which is the one piece of this page you simply have
to be told.

A useful unifying intuition is that は singles something out as the frame against
which the rest is understood. Sometimes that frame is a neutral topic; sometimes
the alternatives become prominent and the result is contrast.

When は follows another particle, the same framing can limit the scope of the
statement. Three sections, one act, weighted differently each time.

**The choice is not "which particle marks the subject" — that question already
picks the wrong axis.** Decide what the sentence is ABOUT (は) versus which
element ANSWERS the implicit question on the table (が), and mark that
instead.

@@ tldr

は is not itself a subject-case marker. It tells the listener to take the
preceding phrase as the topic or frame — and that topic may also correspond to
the sentence's subject, object, time, place or something broader still.

@@ topic — what the sentence is about

**Form:** `N + は` · a phrase that would ordinarily take が or を normally drops
it: 私が → 私は · 本を → 本は

- **The topic can be almost any part of the sentence:**
    - 私は学生です — I'm a student. The topic is also the subject.
    - この店はコーヒーがおいしい — the coffee at this place is good. A topic,
      and a separate が-marked subject.
    - 今日は忙しい — today, I'm busy. The topic is a time.
- **What は fixes is the frame, not the grammar** — it tells you what the comment
  is about. English has no particle for that and has to smuggle the frame into a
  possessive or a prepositional phrase.
- **Once a topic is established, it usually goes unsaid** — Japanese leaves it
  out of later sentences for as long as the intended topic stays clear. It comes
  back when the conversation needs to reorient the listener.
- **は itself carries no register** — 私は学生です works in casual and polite
  speech alike; formality comes from the predicate, だ versus です, not from は.

```ex
この店[みせ]はコーヒーがおいしい。
en: The coffee at this place is good.
lit: as for this place, the coffee is good
note: The sentence needs both particles and they are not competing: この店 says
  what is under discussion, コーヒー is what is good.
bd: この店|this place / は*|as for / コーヒー|coffee / が|(subject) / おいしい|is good
pat: topic は over a separate が-subject
```
```dialog
note: what licenses the unspoken topic — B keeps talking about the place A named without repeating は.
A: この店[みせ]、知[し]ってる？ | Do you know this place?
B: うん、コーヒーがすごくおいしいよ。 | Yeah, the coffee's really good.
```

### は names the topic; が answers the question on the table

が often identifies the clause's subject, while は identifies the discourse
frame. A question and its answer make the difference audible.

誰が来た？ asks who came, and is answered ハルが来た — Haru came — here the
requested subject is also the sentence's focus, which is what が supplies.

ハルは来た is a grammatical sentence, but it answers a different question —
something closer to: what about Haru?

This is also why stories open one way and continue another.
昔々、おじいさんがいました — once upon a time there was an old man — puts him
into the conversation with が.

おじいさんは山へ行きました — the old man went to the mountains — then talks
about him with は. Without a special contrastive or focusing context, reversing
them would sound marked.

!!! note "Subject and topic are different jobs"
    **Subject** is a grammatical relation inside the clause. **Topic** is the
    frame the speaker chooses for organising the message.

    The same noun may serve both — 私は学生です — or the sentence may carry a
    topic and a separate が-marked subject: この店はコーヒーがおいしい.

    In the formal vocabulary, が is a case particle while は sits above the case
    system. That is what lets は attach after other particles in the scope
    section, and why a topic can be a time or a place as easily as a subject.

!!! warning "Pitfall"
    When the question word itself is the unknown subject, use が: 誰が来た？, not
    誰は来た？.

    In the answer too, ハルが作った is the neutral one — Haru made it.
    ハルは作った shifts toward Haru made it, at least, as opposed to someone
    else. The English "Haru did" hides the distinction completely.

### は does not survive inside a relative clause

An ordinary sentence topic normally does not survive as は inside a
noun-modifying (relative) clause. The subject there is usually marked with が —
and sometimes with の.

ハルが作ったケーキ and ハルの作ったケーキ are both the cake Haru made; ハルは
in that slot is not how the clause is built.

-> Compare: [[no-modifier#relative-clause-subject]] — the の that substitutes for が inside a noun-modifying clause
-> Continue with: [[meishishuushokusetsu]] — how a noun-modifying clause is built, and why its subject behaves differently

A contrastive は can appear inside a noun-modifying clause where a genuine local
contrast is intended, so treat this as a strong default rather than an absolute
ban. It is still a useful check when a sentence has run long and lost its shape.

@@ contrast — singling something out

**Form:** `X は …、Y は …` · a single `N は` carrying stress

- **Topic and contrast use the same particle and shade into one another** — a
  neutral topic establishes a frame; contrastive は makes the alternatives,
  stated or unstated, matter to the message.
- **A neutral topic need not invite comparison** — 象は鼻が長い — an elephant's
  nose is long — is not particularly a remark about other animals. Contrast is
  what happens when the alternatives are made to count.
- **The minimal pair is the clearest demonstration** — お酒を飲まない — I don't
  drink alcohol — is neutral object marking, against お酒は飲まない — alcohol,
  I don't drink — which selects alcohol against the alternatives.
- **The unsaid half can do the work** — 犬は好き。猫はちょっと…… — I like
  dogs, cats though — sets the two against each other with は and lets the
  sentence stop before the negative arrives.

```ex
お酒[さけ]は飲[の]まない。
en: Alcohol, I don't drink.
lit: as for alcohol, at least, don't drink
note: An object would ordinarily take を. Swapping in は is what selects alcohol
  against whatever else the speaker might drink, and nothing in the conversation
  has to have raised it first.
bd: お酒|alcohol / は*|as for, at least / 飲まない|don't drink
pat: contrastive は displacing を
```
```dialog
note: what licenses the second は — B's question puts alcohol on the table, and A's answer sets sake against beer with は on both.
A: お酒[さけ]、飲[の]む？ | Do you drink alcohol?
B: ビールは飲[の]むけど、日本酒[にほんしゅ]は苦手[にがて]。 | I drink beer, but I'm not a fan of sake.
```

### Negatives push は toward contrast

Denying one thing all but names the things you are not denying, so negation and
contrast sit together comfortably. お酒は飲まない leaves the door open on
everything else in the fridge.

Add コーヒーは飲む, I do drink coffee, and the pair becomes explicit.

Treat a negative as a **clue** rather than a decision, though. It raises the odds
that a は is contrastive; it does not settle the reading on its own.

### Contrast reading depends on four cues

Most of the time you do not have to choose. The reading sits on a slope rather
than in two boxes, and the same written sentence can be heard either way
depending on how much weight the speaker puts on the は.

Four things push it toward contrast: a second は in the sentence, audible stress,
a negative verb, and a conversation that has already put the alternative on the
table.

With none of the four present, read it as a neutral topic. In a quick reply, は
alone on a fragment still carries the contrast: 私は？ — and me? — asks what
applies to the speaker, everything else left understood.

@@ scope — は stacked on another particle

**Form:** `particle + は` — では · には · へは · からは · までは · とは

- **The first particle stays and goes on doing its own work** — with で, に, へ,
  から, まで and と, は attaches after the complete phrase and marks that whole
  phrase as the topic or contrastive frame.
- **What that most often produces is a limit** — here, for this person, from
  today onward, up to this point. It is a frequent pragmatic effect rather than
  something the grammar entails.
- **Naming the frame invites the comparison** — ここでは吸えません — you can't
  smoke here — makes this place the frame of the rule, and can set it against
  other places.
- **On a scale, the frame is a point rather than a place** — ここまでは分かる
  — I follow it this far — and 今日からは — from today on — fence off what
  lies on the other side.
- **The same stacking appears on signs and notices** — 当店は禁煙です — this
  store is non-smoking — fronts the store as the topic the whole notice is
  about.

```ex
ここでは吸[す]えません。
en: You can't smoke here.
lit: at this place, smoking isn't possible
note: The は makes this location the frame of the rule and can contrast it with
  other locations. On a notice, a contextual impossibility of this kind is
  normally understood as a prohibition rather than a report.
bd: ここ|here / では*|at this place / 吸えません|can't smoke
pat: particle + は, framing the rule
```

### Common combinations

Not an exhaustive list — these are the ones you meet constantly.

| particle | with は | what happens |
|---|---|---|
| が | 〜は | normally absent when the phrase is topicalised |
| を | 〜は | normally absent when the phrase is topicalised |
| に | には | remains before は |
| へ | へは | remains before は |
| で | では | remains before は |
| と | とは | remains before は |
| から | からは | remains before は |
| まで | までは | remains before は |

が and を are the two that go unpronounced. The rest keep expressing their normal
relationship, and は is added on top of the finished phrase.

!!! note "ではない, and where じゃ comes from"
    In the negative copular construction, ではない routinely shortens to じゃない
    in speech — that is where the everyday じゃない comes from, and it is the
    same では.

    じゃあ, それじゃ and じゃまた are related conversational developments from
    では and それでは. They come from the same contraction, but they are
    discourse markers rather than instances of the scope reading above.
