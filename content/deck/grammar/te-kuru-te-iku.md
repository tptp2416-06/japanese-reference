くる and いく start as verbs of physical movement — くる, come, moves toward the speaker; いく, go, moves away. Attach either to a te-form, and that same direction attaches to an action: 買ってくる — go buy something and come back — 持っていく — take something away with you.

The same directions extend from space into time. Once the verb names a change rather than a location, てくる and ていく stop describing where someone walked and start describing which way a change is moving relative to now: toward the present, or forward past it.

Ask which side of now the sentence is pointing at. A change moving toward the present, closing in on where you are, takes てくる; a change or an action continuing onward from here takes ていく.

@@ tldr

てくる and ていく extend て with direction: くる toward the speaker, いく away.
Applied to a change rather than a location, that same direction becomes temporal — てきた for a change arriving at now, ていく for something continuing forward from now.
行ってくる is the fixed way to announce leaving with the promise of return.

@@ directional — physical movement toward the speaker

**Form:** `V-て + くる`

- **てくる adds a return toward the speaker's location onto a verb** — 買ってくる — buy it and bring it back — reports both the errand and its return.
- **Without てくる, the return is left open** — 図書館に行く — going to the library — makes no promise about when or whether the speaker comes back; 図書館に行ってくる makes the round trip part of the sentence.
- **持ってくる brings an object toward the speaker; 連れてくる brings a person** — both attach the same return-toward-here direction, just to different kinds of things being brought.

```eg
プレゼントを買[か]ってくる。 | I'll go buy a present [and come back with it].
```

@@ change-forward — ていく, continuing onward from here

**Form:** `V-て + いく`

- **The physical sense carries an object or a decision away from the speaker** — 持っていく — take it with you — 置いていく — leave it behind [as you go] — both move something away from where the speaker currently is.
- **Applied to an ongoing effort, ていく reframes it as a continuing process rather than a single completed action** — やっていく — keep doing it, going forward — 覚えていく — pick it up bit by bit, going forward — treats the doing as a throughline rather than a task with a finish line.

The difference shows most clearly next to plain ばいい. やればいい points at one sufficient action: do this, and that's enough. やっていけばいい points at a sufficient ongoing process instead: keep doing this, and that's enough — no single action closes it out.

```eg
single sufficient action: やればいい。* | Just do it.
sufficient ongoing process: やっていけばいい。* | Just keep doing it — that's enough.
```

```dialog
note: B reframes A's one-time worry as an ongoing, sustainable process rather than a single task to get right immediately.
A: 全部[ぜんぶ]覚[おぼ]えないと、と思[おも]って。 | I feel like I have to memorize everything.
B: 少[すこ]しずつ覚[おぼ]えていけばいいよ。 | You can just pick it up little by little, going forward.
```

@@ gradual-change — てくる, a change arriving at now

**Form:** `V-て + くる` (grammaticalized, attached to a change verb or an adjective + なる)

- **The same toward-here direction, applied to a change instead of a location, reads as that change approaching the present** — 寒くなってきた — it's getting cold — presents the cold as something that has been arriving, reaching the speaker's present moment rather than sitting fully in the past or fully in the future.
- **慣れてきた — I'm getting used to it — reports the same kind of approach, applied to an internal adjustment rather than the weather.**

The physical and the temporal senses sit on one ladder, not as two unrelated ideas: 買ってくる — go buy something and come back — is the plain spatial sense, the kind of てくる taught first; 慣れてきた — coming to be used to it — is the same toward-here direction, grammaticalized onto an internal change instead of a location. Seeing the ladder whole is what makes the abstract sense feel like an extension of the concrete one rather than a coincidence of spelling.

This is also where てくる and ている part ways on the same material. 寒くなっている presents the cold as a settled state, already in effect — the arriving is over, and what's left is the state. 寒くなってきた presents the same cold as still arriving, the process of getting there still felt in the sentence.

```eg
settled state: 寒[さむ]くなっている。* | It's [now] cold.
change still arriving: 寒[さむ]くなってきた。* | It's getting cold.
```

-> Compare: [[te-iru#resultant-state]] — the settled state a change left behind, as opposed to the change still arriving here

@@ itte-kuru — 行ってくる, leaving with the promise of return

**Form:** `行く + てくる` (lexicalized as a fixed expression)

- **行ってくる is what you say leaving the house** — go, and come back — announcing the trip and the return in one fixed phrase, rather than a productive combination built fresh each time.
- **The reply 行ってらっしゃい answers it as a set pair** — the two exist as a matched departure-and-send-off ritual, not as ordinary sentences built from scratch.

```dialog
note: The departure-and-send-off pair is fixed; neither line is built fresh from the grammar each time.
A: 行[い]ってきます。 | I'm off [and I'll be back].
B: 行[い]ってらっしゃい。 | Take care — see you later.
```

### In conversation

てくる and ていく both compress in fast speech: 持ってく — take it — for 持っていく, やってく — go ahead and do it — for やっていく. The compression drops く's い the same way ている drops its own い in てる — a familiar contraction pattern, applied to a different verb.
