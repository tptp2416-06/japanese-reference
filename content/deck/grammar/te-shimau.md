Japanese has a way of marking that something is finished, all the way, with no walking it back.

メールを送った — I sent the email — simply reports the sending. メールを送ってしまった — I went and sent the email — reports the same event, but with something added: it's done, and there's no taking it back.

That "done, no taking it back" feeling isn't always the same flavor.

Finish something you wanted finished, and it can read as relief or satisfaction. Have something happen that you didn't want, and the same ending reads as regret.

Both are one auxiliary doing one job — marking an ending that can't be walked back — landing differently depending on what got ended.

The sections below start with the full form, てしまう, because that's where the grammar is easiest to see whole.

Say it in an actual conversation, though, and it rarely comes out that way — 送ってしまった becomes 送っちゃった, said the same way, just faster. ちゃう, the contracted form, is what you'll actually hear.

It comes right after てしまう's own sections here, not because it matters less, but because a contraction is easiest to learn once you've seen what it's contracting.

@@ tldr

てしまう (usually said as ちゃう) marks an action as finished — completely, and with no taking it back.
Whether that lands as satisfaction or as regret depends on what got finished, not on a different grammar point.
Ask: am I just reporting what happened, or framing it as done and irreversible? That's the choice this auxiliary makes.

@@ completion

**Form:** `V-て + しまう` (uncontracted; the form ちゃう contracts from)

- **The uncontracted form leans formal or written** — narration, notices, and careful speech reach for しまう where a text between friends would contract it to ちゃう.
- **Completion-flavored しまう often comes with a deadline or a goal** — it frames finishing as getting all the way through something, not as an accident.
- **Some verbs resist it** — ある, いる, わかる describe a standing state rather than an event, so there's rarely a "finish" for しまう to mark.

てしまう rarely gets negated to mean "won't end up finishing." If nothing got finished, there's nothing left for the auxiliary to report — so Japanese usually just drops it and negates the plain verb instead.

One shape shows up anyway: V-てしまわないと. It doesn't deny that the action gets finished.

Instead, と turns the whole clause into an elliptical "if this doesn't happen, trouble" — the completion sense of しまう sits inside that conditional, rather than being negated by it.

```eg
明日までにレポートを書いてしまわないと。 | I have to get the report written by tomorrow.
```

-> Prerequisite: [[nakereba-naranai#nai-to]] — the same elliptical "if this doesn't happen, trouble" pattern しまわないと borrows

A different auxiliary points the opposite way: ておく sets something up now so it's ready later, while てしまう marks something as already, finally finished.

The two often turn up in the same story — one preparing what the other later disrupts or completes.

-> Contrast: [[te-oku]] — the forward-looking partner: getting something ready in advance, not marking it as finished

Register climbs further still. A notice might read 締め切ってしまいます — we'll be closing applications — plain てしまう, dressed in the polite -ます ending, doing formal work without becoming a different construction.

@@ regret

**Form:** `V-て + しまう` (same uncontracted form as completion; here regret or adversity is what colors it)

- **Regret-flavored しまう usually attaches to something unwanted** — a mistake, a loss, a turn of events nobody asked for — framed the same way completion is: done, and no taking it back.
- **The speaker doesn't have to be at fault** — 電車が遅れてしまった — the train ended up running late — frames the lateness as an unwelcome, unavoidable done deal, with nobody in the sentence causing it.

```ex
大事な写真を消してしまった。
en: I deleted an important photo.
lit: an important photo, went and erased
note: てしまう doesn't just report the deleting — it frames it as complete and impossible to undo, and that framing is what makes this land as regret.
bd: 大事な写真を|the important photo / 消してしまった*|ended up erasing
pat: V-てしまう, an ending that can't be walked back
```

!!! warning "English Trap"
    The textbook gloss "regret" makes it tempting to hear てしまう as an apology built into the grammar. That reflex mistranslates a neutral, even happy completion as if the speaker were sorry.

    一気に全部読んでしまった can just mean "I read the whole thing in one sitting," said with satisfaction, not guilt. The regret comes from what got finished, not from てしまう itself.

A different construction reaches a similar unwanted feeling by another route: the adversity passive makes the affected person the sentence's subject, rather than adding an auxiliary onto the original verb.

-> Compare: [[rareru#adversity-passive]] — the same unwanted-outcome feeling, reached by making the affected person the subject instead

@@ chau

**Form:** `V-て + しまう → V-ちゃう` · `V-で + しまう → V-じゃう`

- **ちゃう is simply how the てしまう you just saw actually gets said** — in writing and careful speech it stays uncontracted; conversation collapses it onto the verb's て-form almost every time.
- **The contraction is mechanical** — て before しまう becomes ちゃ: 食べてしまう becomes 食べちゃう.
- **で-verbs voice the same way** — で before しまう becomes じゃ, carrying over the voicing で already had: 遊んでしまう becomes 遊んじゃう.
- **It attaches to the whole event, not to a single word in the sentence** — there's no way to mark just the object as finished; ちゃう scopes over the whole verb phrase.

!!! warning "Pitfall"
    Because ちゃう sounds so casual, it's tempting to treat it as a looser, separate construction from てしまう — something to swap out for a "real" grammar point once the conversation turns polite.

    They're one construction at two registers, not two different ones. Going polite uncontracts it rather than swapping in a different ending.

    忘れちゃった — I went and forgot it — becomes 忘れてしまいました — I'm afraid I forgot it. The completion-or-regret meaning travels across unchanged.

The choice that matters is not which flavor to reach for. It's whether to mark the event as finished and irreversible at all — reach for ちゃう when that's the point, and leave it off for a plain report of what happened.

```eg
返信、もう出しちゃった。 | Already sent the reply.
```

@@ chatta

**Form:** `V-ちゃった` (past)

- **ちゃった is where completion and regret actually show up** — most real てしまう/ちゃう sentences are in the past, because "done and irreversible" is usually a claim about something that already happened.
- **The same ending covers both a satisfied "finally done" and a rueful "oh no"** — nothing in ちゃった itself picks a side; what got finished does.

```eg
relief: 宿題、全部終わっちゃった。 | I got all the homework done.
regret: 傘を電車に忘れちゃった。 | I left my umbrella on the train.
```

```dialog
note: what licenses ちゃった — A has already spotted the stain, so B's ちゃった owns the mishap rather than introducing it.
A: あれ、シャツに何かついてるよ。 | Hey, there's something on your shirt.
B: ああ、コーヒーこぼしちゃった。 | Ugh, I spilled coffee.
```

@@ chaou

**Form:** `V-ちゃおう` (the volitional しまおう, contracted)

- **ちゃおう commits to finishing something now, briskly** — where ちゃう and ちゃった describe what happens or happened, ちゃおう is the speaker deciding to get it done.
- **It often carries a "might as well" tone** — the completion is framed as something worth clearing out of the way, not lingered over.

-> Prerequisite: [[ikoukei]] — the plain volitional しまおう that ちゃおう contracts from

```dialog
note: what licenses ちゃおう — B turns down the offer to wait and commits to finishing right away.
A: 掃除、明日でいいよ。 | The cleaning can wait until tomorrow.
B: ううん、今やっちゃおう。 | No, let's just get it done now.
```

@@ chau-kamo

**Form:** `V-ちゃう + かも`

- **ちゃうかも guesses at an unwanted or unplanned finish** — pairing the contraction with かも's casual conjecture means the speaker isn't sure yet, only that it might end up happening.
- **The uncertainty belongs to かも, not to ちゃう** — ちゃう still claims the event would be complete and irreversible if it happens; かも is what keeps the whole thing hypothetical.

-> Prerequisite: [[kamo]] — the casual conjecture ちゃうかも borrows its uncertainty from

```eg
返信、忘れちゃうかも。 | I might end up forgetting to reply.
```

Line the whole family up by how formal it sounds, and the difference the ear can't skip becomes visible:

| form | example | effect | how it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| てしまった | 財布を忘れてしまった — I went and forgot my wallet | reports the slip as a finished, unwalked-back fact | neutral — plain speech or writing |
| ちゃった | 財布を忘れちゃった — I forgot my wallet | the same slip, said the way you'd actually say it out loud | very common in conversation |
| [[te-shimaimashita#polite]] | 財布を忘れてしまいました — I'm afraid I forgot my wallet | the same slip, said formally | deferential — polite speech or writing |

### In conversation

しまった alone stands as a whole reaction, no verb needed, because しまう itself does the reacting: しまった！ — Oh no!

And once the rest is obvious, a whole clause shrinks to just the ending: もう食べちゃった？ — Already eaten?

The reply shrinks the same way: 食べちゃった。 — Yeah, already did.
